How GPS Works — The Big Picture

🛰️

1. Satellites Broadcast

Each GPS satellite continuously broadcasts its precise orbital position (ephemeris) and the exact time from its onboard atomic clock. These signals travel at the speed of light.

📡

2. Receiver Measures Delay

Your GPS receiver picks up signals from multiple satellites. By comparing the broadcast time with the arrival time, it calculates the pseudorange — the approximate distance to each satellite.

📍

3. Trilateration

With distances from 4+ satellites, the receiver solves a system of equations to find its exact latitude, longitude, altitude, and correct its own clock error.

The Fundamental Equation

di = c × (treceiver − tsatellite_i)
Where d = pseudorange, c = speed of light (299,792,458 m/s), and t = time
1 nanosecond error = 0.3m position error 1 microsecond error = 300m position error

Why 4 Satellites?

In 2D, 3 circles can uniquely identify a point. In 3D, you need 4 spheres — 3 for spatial coordinates (x, y, z) plus 1 extra to solve for the receiver's clock bias, since consumer GPS receivers don't have atomic clocks.

About WGS-84

The World Geodetic System 1984 is the reference frame used by GPS. It models Earth as an oblate spheroid with semi-major axis a = 6,378,137 m and flattening f = 1/298.257223563.
